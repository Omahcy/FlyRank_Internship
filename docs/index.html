Which pages to review first: query patterns associated with search decline

Oluomachukwu Emenike. Capstone, Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring lane. FlyRank AI ML Internship, 2026.

Abstract

Editors with limited time need a way to decide which pages to review when search visibility falls. I asked whether simple search-query patterns can help rank pages that lose more than 20% of their impressions from one 30-day window to the next. Using about 102,000 pages from 49 clients in the FlyRank ML Internship dataset, I trained a random forest on five features and validated it with splits that keep each client entirely in training or entirely in test. The model carries only a weak signal (ROC-AUC 0.64 across held-out clients) and does not beat a majority-class baseline on accuracy, but a two-code rule, “one query dominates” and “many hidden queries,” separates pages that declined 68.0% of the time from pages with neither code at 60.2%. The results are observed and directional, and suit coarse triage for human review, not prediction or causal claims.

Introduction

A content team cannot review every page. When a page’s impressions fall, someone has to decide whether to refresh it, watch it, or leave it alone. The decision this paper supports is narrow: given a list of pages, which should an editor open first?

I focus on one family of signals: how a page’s visibility is spread across search queries. A page that depends on a single query behaves differently from one that earns impressions across many. The question is whether those patterns are associated with decline strongly enough, and consistently enough across clients, to be useful.

Data

The data is the FlyRank ML Internship dataset (the full warehouse release). I used the daily search-performance table, the 90-day query table, and the content dimension table. The latest date in the release anchors two windows: the last 30 days and the 30 days before them.

  • Unit of analysis: one page. All client and page identifiers in the release are hashed, and none appear in this paper.
  • Minimum history: pages needed at least 100 impressions in the earlier 30-day window, so a percentage change means something. This gave 111,247 pages.
  • Missing features: pages without query features were dropped, leaving 102,203 pages. The per-client splits contain 49 clients.
  • Outcome: 63.3% of the 102,203 pages declined, so the classes are unbalanced.

Methodology

Label

A page is declining if its impressions in the last 30 days are below 80% of its impressions in the previous 30 days. The 20% threshold is a judgment call, not a standard.

Features

Five features: previous-30-day impressions, the number of visible queries, the share of impressions from rare queries, the share from anonymized (hidden) queries, and the share held by the single top query.

Baseline

The baseline always predicts the majority class. A model has to beat it on the same split to show it has learned anything.

Validation design

The starting point was a random split, which lets pages from the same client appear in both training and test. I replaced it with splits grouped by client, using GroupShuffleSplit for repeated hold-outs and GroupKFold for out-of-client scoring. Models were small random forests (60 trees, depth 12, at least 50 pages per leaf), so repeated runs finish quickly.

Leakage checks

  • Previous-30-day impressions feed the label, so I tested that feature alone. It scored no better than chance (ROC-AUC 0.496), so the model is not just recovering the label’s arithmetic.
  • Reason-code cut-offs were learned on one half of the clients and applied to the other half.
  • Query features come from a 90-day table that overlaps the label window. This cannot be removed and is a limitation, covered below.

Results

The model barely beats the baseline, and not across clients

The per-client score is lower, so part of the first result came from patterns shared by pages of the same client. Across three seeds, ROC-AUC was 0.658 (standard deviation 0.003) under a random split and 0.635 (0.031) under a per-client split. Both are above 0.5, so a weak signal exists, but the per-client number varies a lot with which clients are held out.

The query features carry most of the signal

Two reason codes survive a held-out test

In the full data, three reason codes looked useful. When their cut-offs were learned on half of the clients and applied to the other 25, one held up strongly (“one query dominates”: 69.2% of flagged pages declined against 60.0% of others), one weakly (“many hidden queries”: 64.6% against 62.1%), and one not at all (“many rare queries”: 62.2% against 62.9%). I dropped the third and repeated the test over 10 random client splits with the remaining two.

Limitations

  • Overlapping windows. The query features cover 90 days, including the 30 days that define decline. They are contemporaneous correlates of decline, not forecasts made beforehand.
  • Choices made after inspection. The reason codes and the decision to drop one were made after looking at the data, so the held-out tests are only partly independent.
  • Few clients. With 49 clients, results shift depending on which are held out.
  • Arbitrary threshold. Results may change with a different cut-off than 20%. Seasonal drops are not separated from real losses.
  • No causal claims. The results are observed, directional and for decision support. They do not show that any pattern causes a decline, and they say nothing about how Google’s algorithm works.

Ranked recommendations

Use these as a review order for a human editor. None of them is an automatic action.

Review first: both risk codes

A single query dominates the page’s visibility and many queries are hidden. About 68% of such pages declined on held-out clients. Check how the page appears for its main query, whether the title and snippet still match what searchers want, and whether the content is out of date.

Review next: one risk code

About 66.5% declined. A dominant query is the stronger of the two codes, so start there. Look for a single point of dependence and consider whether the page covers related queries.

Monitor: no risk code

About 60.2% declined, the lowest rate but still well above half. Keep watching these pages and review them only if their trend continues.

Within a tier the ordering is noisy, so editors should add their own judgment about a page’s business value.
